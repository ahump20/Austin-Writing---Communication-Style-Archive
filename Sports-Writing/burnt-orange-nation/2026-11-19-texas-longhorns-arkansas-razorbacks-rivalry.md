Texas fans do not need to pretend Arkansas is Oklahoma or Texas A&M.

They also do not need to pretend Arkansas is nobody.

Somewhere between those positions is an honest answer to the question that follows the Razorbacks into another meeting with the Longhorns — does this rivalry still matter?

Yes. But asking younger Texas fans to feel what their grandparents felt is a poor way to explain why.

The Longhorns, No. 5 in the College Football Playoff rankings released Tuesday, host the Razorbacks on [Saturday at 2:30 p.m. Central](https://texaslonghorns.com/sports/football/schedule/2026) on ESPN. It will be the 82nd meeting in a series that began in 1894 and that Texas lead 58-23.

A rivalry needs repetition. There has to be another game coming, another chance to settle something, another season spent hearing about the last one. For much of the past three decades, Texas and Arkansas had the history without the habit.

The teams played in 60 consecutive seasons through 1991. Then Arkansas left the Southwest Conference for the Southeastern Conference, and a fixture became an occasional reunion.

Between that final SWC meeting and Texas’ arrival in the SEC in 2024, they played six times. Six games across more than three decades. Entire college careers passed without a Texas-Arkansas game. Fans could grow up, graduate. and start families while waiting for the next one. A Texas fan who turns 35 this year was born the year of that last Southwest Conference meeting.

That is a scheduling explanation for the distance some Texas fans feel, not a character flaw.

#### The history deserves better than a highlight reel

There is plenty to inherit.

Arkansas’ 14-13 victory in Austin in 1964 helped shape the Razorbacks’ national championship season. Five years later, No. 1 Texas and No. 2 Arkansas met in Fayetteville with President Richard Nixon in attendance. The Longhorns erased a 14-point deficit and won 15-14, then beat Notre Dame in the Cotton Bowl to complete an undefeated season.

Those games matter because of what the programs took from each other and what they had to survive to get there. The stakes were earned before anybody needed a television package to explain them.

But the 1969 game cannot sustain the rivalry forever.

If every explanation of why Arkansas matters begins and ends with Darrell Royal, Frank Broyles, and Richard Nixon, it should not be surprising when someone born decades later treats the rivalry as an old family story. A good one, perhaps. Still someone else’s.

That generational distance even surfaced in a joke this week. According to posts by the [Austin American-Statesman’s Danny Davis](https://x.com/_dannydavis/status/2122405128292503838) and [Inside Texas’ Evan Vieth](https://x.com/EvanVieth/status/2122405050584543308), center Connor Robertson had begun answering a question from venerable Houston Chronicle columnist Kirk Bohls about how far back the rivalry goes when quarterback Arch Manning interjected with a crack about Bohls’ childhood.

It is a funny way to arrive at a real problem: For current players, the rivalry’s defining moments happened long before their own football lives began. That does not make the history irrelevant but it does mean history needs some help.

The more useful question is what Texas and Arkansas have done to each other since.

Enough that dismissing the series requires some selective memory.

#### The modern meetings were rarely forgettable

Arkansas won four of the six games played while the programs occupied different conferences — the 2000 Cotton Bowl, the 2003 meeting in Austin, the 2014 Texas Bowl, and the 2021 game in Fayetteville. Texas answered in 2004 and 2008. Add the Longhorns’ wins in 2024 and 2025, and the eight meetings since Arkansas left the SWC are split four apiece.

The distances between those games made the rivalry less familiar. The results did little to make it comfortable.

The 40-21 loss in 2021 came in Steve Sarkisian’s second game at Texas. Whatever expectations accompanied the new coaching staff into Fayetteville, the Razorbacks offered a blunt assessment of how much work remained.

Sarkisian [said Monday](https://texaslonghorns.com/news/2026/11/16/football-video-steve-sarkisian-press-conference-nov-16) he doesn’t forget it. “I’m well aware of the rivalry,” he said, recalling the trip to Fayetteville, “being a ranked team and losing and them storming the field.”

Texas has since won the first two SEC meetings, 20-10 in Fayetteville in 2024 and 52-37 in Austin last season. Those victories belong in the rivalry’s present, too. Arkansas does not become more legitimate when Texas loses or less legitimate when Texas wins.

Nor does a rivalry require equal attention from both sides.

The SEC’s schedule makes the same point. When the league assigned annual opponents for 2026 through 2029, [Texas drew Oklahoma, Texas A&M and Arkansas](https://texaslonghorns.com/news/2025/9/23/footballs-2026-29-sec-opponents-announced). [Arkansas drew LSU, Missouri and Texas](https://arkansasrazorbacks.com/hogs-2026-2029-sec-opponents-unveiled/). The same game is on both lists, but it does not hold the same place on each.

A Texas fan can care more about Oklahoma and Texas A&M without stripping Arkansas of its place. Ranking the people you want to beat is different from deciding everyone outside the top two is interchangeable.

Arkansas has earned more than that. The Longhorns have experienced enough expensive reminders.

This week has the ingredients for another. Texas [opened as a 20.5-point favorite](https://www.burntorangenation.com/general/107274/no-6-texas-opens-as-heavy-favorite-over-arkansas) over a 4-6 team with a first-year head coach in Ryan Silverfield. That team also beat then-No. 17 South Carolina 34-6 last week. And while the Longhorns are 5-0 this season against teams that were ranked when they played them, both of their losses came against teams that were not: Oklahoma and Florida, each of which has since climbed into the playoff rankings.

Sarkisian’s message this week made room for both the history and the responsibility of playing the next game. Discussing how Texas avoids a letdown, he pointed to Brad Spence and Ian Geffrard as players who understand the Arkansas locker room’s investment in this matchup. That perspective can reach teammates who were born well after the demise of the Southwest Conference. Sarkisian also brought up Royal and Broyles and said that Ted Koy, a halfback on the 1969 team, is at practice every day and talks to the players about the importance of this game.

Still, Sarkisian argued that understanding the rivalry cannot be the only reason Texas performs well. The Longhorns also have to demonstrate that their preparation holds up from one week to the next.

“I think this is a good test for us, right, to show that level of maturity,” Sarkisian said.

That is a more useful assignment than demanding a particular amount of hatred. Learn why the opponent cares. Then prepare well enough to keep that opponent from making you care for all the wrong reasons.

#### Let the next generation make its own case

The most encouraging development for this rivalry is also the least romantic — the teams are scheduled to keep playing.

Under the SEC’s announced 2026-29 rotation, Arkansas and Texas are annual opponents. Saturday makes three straight seasons with a meeting, which had not happened since 1989-91, and the series is set to alternate between Austin and Fayetteville through 2029. The league has said annual opponents will be evaluated after each four-year cycle, so nothing beyond that is promised. Still, it gives the series a chance to recover the ordinary repetition that made its extraordinary games possible.

A student who arrived on campus in 2024 will leave having seen Arkansas four times, one more than Texas fans got in the 19 seasons from 2005 through 2023 combined.

A missed tackle can carry into next November. A road loss can wait for a return visit. Players can learn what the game feels like instead of being told what it used to mean.

The schedule cannot manufacture affection or animosity, but it can give both somewhere to grow.

That is why this rivalry still matters. It connects Texas to a substantial part of its football history while leaving room for something more useful than reenactment. The next meaningful Texas-Arkansas game does not have to resemble 1969. It can matter to the people playing and watching it now.

Let Oklahoma be Oklahoma. Let Texas A&M be Texas A&M.

Arkansas has its own history with Texas, and finally, another game on the calendar to do something with it.
